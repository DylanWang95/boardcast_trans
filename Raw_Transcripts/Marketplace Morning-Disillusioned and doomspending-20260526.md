Marketplace's Rima Kreis, host of This is Uncomfortable, explains the impulse and what to do about it. It comes down to feeling disillusioned with our economy. At least that's what Chelsea Fagan, the co-founder of The Financial Diet, hears from her readers and listeners. The most common question we get by far is like, why should I have a retirement account? No one's ever going to retire. Whether that's for climate reasons or political reasons, it's sort of that way of thinking, imagining there is no future and becoming completely defeatist about it. Defeatist about the high cost of living, a cooling job market, the burden of student debt. In a new Bank of America survey, 42% of Gen Z adults say they're living paycheck to paycheck. Fagan says that desperation can make spending money feel weirdly rational, like a small way to get relief or control. But we so often feel like these smaller, often kind of self-defeating purchases are making us feel better or are a better use of our time while we're here. And I actually think that the vast majority of them are just not that great. That pull toward the immediate, especially when the future feels shaky, is what behavioral economists call present bias. Where you basically elevate the present, the spending you're able to do in the present, over the future. Judd Kessler, an economist at the Wharton Business School, says you might tell yourself, OK, next year I'm going to start saving for retirement. But he says studies show that if you want to get people to start thinking more of their future selves. You have to make the future self and the present self like more connected.
